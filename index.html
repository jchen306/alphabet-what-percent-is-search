Alphabet: What Percent Is Search

Nothing is less productive than to make more efficient what should not be done at all

For Eric Johnston · October 19, 2026 · Justin Chen

1. Introduction

Big companies trade on narratives, and the story around Google is that Search is dead.

Alphabet has grown to what it is today because people had questions, Google had answers, and advertisers wanted a slice of that attention. The core business model, then, ran implicitly on the premise that users could find no better platform or method to find and access information. That held true for 25 years, but it doesn't hold now. ChatGPT launched in December 2022, and three weeks later, Google declared a code red.

We know now the steps Google has since taken: go 100% into AI, from chips, to models, to data centers, to the search box that will unify the three, with the goal to disrupt its own Search business before upstart competitors get the chance to do it themselves. But this begs the question: what happens when Alphabet — the third largest company in the world with infinite knowledge on the American consumer and a balance sheet that rivals the GDP of certain countries — tries to disrupt its core business? What remains and how successful is its replacement? How much is there to cannibalize? This paper investigates these questions.

3. Analyzing Revenue

It is clear that Search remains Alphabet’s core revenue engine, generating $63.3B in Q2 2026, or 52.8% of total revenue. This is unsurprising and unhelpful. A single quarter at relatively familiar levels tells us little about if/how the business is changing and where it's going. Therefore, below, we plot revenue over time, in dollars and in relative percentages.

Excluding Cloud, every other segment of the business shrunk as a percentage of total revenue. Search and Network combined fell 7.6 pts to 58.9%, while Cloud nearly doubled its footprint from 10.7% to 20.7%. This seems to confirm the narrative: the segments of Alphabet that make money from advertising are still growing in dollars and still carry the cash flow, but with every passing quarter the center of gravity of the company shifts more and more toward Cloud and the AI buildout. Even more than this, we additionally note that these numbers actually understate the rate and scale of this transition.

For one, these numbers are built on gross revenue, which treats traffic acquisition costs (TAC) as revenue retained by the company, rather than recognizing it for what it is —money already earmarked to partners like Apple, Samsung, and other wireless carriers that have agreed to host Google as the default search engine on their products.

TAC moves from year to year, but since 2018 it has stayed between a band of 20% and 23% of Google's advertising revenue. Unfortunately, Alphabet now files TAC as one number for Search and Network, but up until Q3 2019 it still reported the split: Network partners kept 69% to 74% of the Network revenue they generated, falling steadily over that stretch, and search distribution partners took 12% to 13% of revenue on Google's own properties. Another factor understating the decline of traditional Search is that Search has included revenue from ads in AI Overviews and AI Mode since May 2024. To measure the extent of expected cannibalization on the legacy business, then, we must net out the incremental contribution due to AI surfaces. We do this by splitting growth into its two filed drivers — paid clicks and cost-per-click — and estimating trends for both.

Search's growth over the past year has come from volume, and that volume has come from AI. If we assume zero cannibalization and that legacy clicks sustained their pre-AI growth rate of 3%, we establish the most generous ceiling for legacy volume growth. The question now becomes how much deterioration to subtract. Google Network provides a starting point. Network sells ads on third-party sites and apps, many of which rely on Google Search for visitors, and it has no AI surfaces. For all four quarters in 2025 its impressions fell 5-6% and then, after AI Mode expanded to more than 200 countries in October 2025, jumped to 9% in Q1 2026 and 12% in Q2. While this is certainly an imperfect control, given its exposure to in-app ads and falling impressions in 2024 (before AI), but the renewed acceleration in 2026 does seem to suggest that AI is continuing to pressure traditional search traffic.

Third-party data points the same way. Pew found users click a traditional result on 8% of visits when an AI summary appears against 15% when it does not, and Ahrefs measured a 58% lower click-through rate on the top result under an AI Overview. AI summaries now appear on about a third of US searches, and losing half the clicks on those searches implies a US decline near 15%. The US is 49% of Search revenue. For the other 51% we assume a 2% decline. AI Mode reached most of those markets only in October 2025, so Q2 2026 is their second full quarter of exposure against the fifth for the US, and the US path over its first two quarters was a single-digit decline. Network impressions show the same lag: they fell 5% to 6% through 2025, when the US had AI Mode and the rest of the world did not, then stepped to 9% and 12% once it went global. Each point of ex-US decline moves the floor about half a point, so the revenue-weighted floor is about −8%. Against the +3% ceiling, our base case of −3% sits near the midpoint.

Price is moving the other way, and most of the move is mix. CPC growth slowed from 8% before AI Mode to 3% in Q2 2026, or about 2% in constant currency. AI Max, used by 500,000 advertisers, is unlocking billions of searches that were hard to monetize, and AI Mode users ask longer, more specific questions on which ads are still in testing. Those incremental clicks are cheaper, so they pull the reported average down even if prices on the traditional page hold. The traditional page keeps the highest-intent commercial queries while losing volume, which can push advertisers to bid more for each remaining click, and Network's 13% rise in cost-per-impression seems to illustrate that pattern.

We model legacy CPC growth of 7% in the bull case, matching the pre-AI rate, 5% in the base, 2% in the bear (the constant-currency blended rate), and zero in the stress case. The range captures the difference between mix and pricing power. Cheaper AI clicks can dilute reported CPC long before traditional prices fall, but if legacy volume keeps eroding, Google will eventually lose the room to keep raising them.

These estimates ultimately rest on a set of assumptions that are too crude to fully appreciate reality and too imprecise to base any real quantitative analysis on. However, what it does offer is clarity to the direction of travel: the traditional results page is no longer an engine of expansion so much as it is a cash base to defend just long enough to fund its successor. It is clear, whether as a share of revenue, profit, or Search, that Google's future will not depend on advertising (at least not through websites and links), but on Cloud, TPUs, and whether they can scale quickly enough to replace the high-margin economics of the legacy Search business they are disrupting. On that question, there are three reasons why we would be surprised if Cloud didn't account for more than half of Alphabet's operating profit by 2028. Beginning with trends in operating income.

Cloud's share of Alphabet's operating income has doubled every year, from 2% in 2023 to 5% in 2024, 11% in 2025 and 22% in Q2 2026, when it earned $8.8B of $40.8B, and 1H26 Cloud operating income of $15.4B already exceeds the $13.6B earned in all of 2025 because profit is growing faster than revenue. Why? The reported operating margin rose from 20.8% in Q2 2025 ($2.8B on $13.6B) to 35.6% in Q2 2026 ($8.8B on $24.8B), yet the incremental margin, which measures how much of each new revenue dollar reaches operating income, was higher still. Between those two quarters revenue grew $11.1B and operating income grew $6.0B, an incremental margin of 54%. Because the new dollars earn 54 cents and the average dollar earns 36, every quarter of growth pulls the reported margin higher, and that is how operating income tripled on 82% revenue growth. One more doubling of share puts Cloud past 40% of Alphabet's operating income.

Second, demand is well in excess of capacity. Backlog went from about $240B at year end 2025 to $462B in Q1 and $514B in Q2, five times the year prior, and the $52B net addition in Q2 came alongside $24.8B of recognized revenue, so Alphabet is still booking roughly three dollars for every dollar it delivers. The shortage is also industry wide. Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet together hold $1.7T of commitments, up 150% on the year, and all three have begun raising prices on some workloads. Alphabet itself has raised the top of its 2026 capex range by $15B because capacity arrived faster than planned, and from September it will begin renting compute from SpaceX at a premium to serve its largest customers, a six month bridge that management says earns a highly positive lifetime return. More than the demand being large and persistent, it is broad. Cloud has 17 product lines above $1B of annual revenue, new customer additions and $100M+ deals have both more than doubled year on year, and AI customers buy 1.8x as many products as the rest.

Finally, Alphabet now sells TPU systems, a second revenue stream on top of renting capacity. It buys the chips and rack components, holds them in inventory and sells finished systems at a gross margin, with the sales entering backlog and flowing through Cloud revenue gross at delivery. The margin therefore lands the quarter the system ships rather than over the multi-year life of an owned asset, and on premise buyers sign multi-year contracts on Google's stack. Customers can also subscribe through the cloud or take capacity through neocloud partners. The business is already large. Press reports describe a single commitment for about one million TPUs at about $35B, roughly 1.3GW of compute and more than a year of current Cloud revenue, and Alphabet has expanded its custom silicon agreement with Marvell to supply it. The market around it is growing faster still, with Broadcom, which designs custom accelerators for Alphabet and other hyperscalers, guiding to about 10GW of customer deployments in FY27 and 20GW in FY28. Systems carry lower margins than software and services, so the segment margin can narrow even as the profit dollars grow.

4. What's next?

We take the 12-month risk-neutral distribution from a strip of $10-wide butterflies. Each butterfly's cost, grown at the risk-free rate and divided by its $10 maximum payoff, is the probability of that band. The strip is priced off a fitted surface with 32% one-year ATM volatility (October 2 quotes were 34.6% at one month and 33.0% at three months, both through the October 28 print), +3.5 volatility points at the 25-delta put and −1.5 at the 25-delta call. The other inputs are r of 3.75%, a dividend yield of 0.25% and spot of $340.18.

At $340 (October 2 close) GOOG trades on 22x the $15.25 consensus 2027 EPS, against 23x forward for the Nasdaq-100. Terminal odds are 24% at or above Cantor's $420 target (+23%, 28x EPS), 22% at or above the $430 Street mean (+26%, 28x), 8% at or above the $515 Street high (+51%, 34x) and 45% below the $340 Street low (22x). One-year volatility between 28% and 36% moves the odds at Cantor's target between 22% and 26% and at the Street high between 6% and 10%.

5. Conclusion

Search is still most of Alphabet, but less of it each quarter. It supplied 52.8% of revenue and 67.0% of Services-plus-Cloud operating profit in Q2 2026, and both shares have fallen steadily since Q1 2024, with profit share falling fastest because Cloud carries no TAC and its margin has nearly quadrupled. What is being cannibalized is the traditional results page. Search grew 16.8% in Q2, but the growth came from paid clicks that arrived with ads in AI Overviews and AI Mode, and our estimates put the page of links at roughly 2% growth once those surfaces are stripped out. Network, the one filed line with no AI offset, is already shrinking, with impressions down 12% and revenue down six quarters running. What is replacing that page is Cloud, which now earns 22% of Alphabet's operating income, converts 54 cents of each new revenue dollar to profit, holds a $514B backlog that management says it is still supply-constrained to serve, and has added TPU system sales as a second revenue line. Our estimates carry wide error bars, but every measure points the same way, and we expect Cloud's share of operating profit to reach about half by 2028. The equity is a bet that Cloud scales fast enough to replace the growth Search gives up, and the market prices that bet at about one chance in four of reaching Cantor's $420 target, which is 28x consensus 2027 EPS, and 45% odds of finishing below today's price. The next test is the Q3 print on October 28.
